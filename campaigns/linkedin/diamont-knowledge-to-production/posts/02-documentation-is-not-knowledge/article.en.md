# Documentation Is Not Knowledge

Most teams do not have too little documentation.

They have too many places to look for it.

Decisions in tickets. Diagrams in shared drives. Business rules in messages. ADRs that no longer reflect the system. Onboarding documents. Code comments. Meeting recordings. Prompts that worked once. And somewhere, a person who still remembers why the original decision was made.

All of it contains information.

But available information is not the same as preserved knowledge.

A document can describe a solution precisely and still lose what matters most: the intent that originated it, the context in which it was valid, the alternatives that were rejected, the authority behind the decision, its relationships, its evidence, and whether it remains current.

Without that structure, a document becomes an isolated piece. It can be read, but not necessarily applied safely.

This explains a familiar experience: a team “has the documentation” but must reconstruct the reasoning whenever a new project, person, or agent arrives.

## Retrieving text is not retrieving knowledge

Search systems and language models can retrieve fragments with extraordinary effectiveness. They can summarize hundreds of pages and identify similarities that would take a person hours to find.

Retrieving a fragment, however, does not prove that it is sufficient, current, or applicable.

Responsible production requires more than semantic proximity. It requires identity, provenance, relationships, authority, and context.

An authorization pattern observed in a financial platform may be highly valuable. Copying its classes to another project does not preserve that value. Even copying its explanation may be insufficient. The reusable virtue may not be the implementation, but the principle it protects.

Preserving knowledge requires separating that virtue from its accidental materialization.

## From documents to Patrimony

DIAMONT uses the idea of a Crystal to explore that separation.

A Crystal is not merely a well-written file. It is knowledge with permanent identity, purpose, provenance, and relationships. It does not belong to a tool merely because it is stored there, and it does not become true because an agent produced it.

Faces express how that knowledge is understood or materialized in different contexts. Compilations can deliver reproducible knowledge sets for a specific production. MINA can explore the Patrimony, discover applicable Crystals, and expose gaps without owning the source of truth.

The goal is not to create a perfect library. It is to help knowledge survive the end of a project, a team change, a technology migration, a different agent, the loss of a vendor, or a need that did not exist when the experience was first documented.

A useful test is simple:

> Can another person—or an agent—use this documentation to make the correct decision without manually reconstructing all the reasoning that originated it?

If not, we may have preserved information, but not yet operational knowledge.

Documentation remains essential. Documents are fundamental materializations: they communicate, transport, and allow people to review understanding.

The mistake is confusing the container with what we intended to preserve.

Documentation records.

Knowledge connects, explains, constrains, and can guide new production.

The challenge is not to write more. It is to make what we learn discoverable, understandable, applicable, and extensible without losing its origin.
